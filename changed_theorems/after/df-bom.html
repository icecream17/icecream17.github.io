Definition df-bom 51381
Description: Define the signed prime Omega function on nonzero rational numbers by summing the nonzero prime valuations pCnt defined in df-pc 17064. The support is finite by pcqfi 51379. Denominator factors contribute negatively. Its value is the prime-factor count of the absolute numerator minus that of the positive denominator, with multiplicity; see bomnumden 51402. Its restriction to the positive integers is the prime Omega function, which counts prime factors with multiplicity; see bomzval 51386. Changing the sign of the input does not change the value (bomneg 51398), and a reciprocal prime has value minus one (bomrec 51397 and bomprm 51389). Thus "signed" describes the denominator contributions, not the sign of the input.

The label fragment "bom" abbreviates "big omega", a name for the prime Omega function. See "Big Omega Function" in M. Billal and A. Parvardi, "Topics in Number Theory: An Olympiad-Oriented Approach", second edition, section 3.7, p. 215 (PDF p. 217), https://parvardi.ca/resources/topics-in-number-theory.pdf#page=217 51389. See also S. Sugden and D. Miller, "Exploring the Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic in Excel 2007", Spreadsheets in Education 4, no. 2, Article 2, section 8, exercises 17-18, p. 23 (PDF p. 25), https://sie.scholasticahq.com/article/4578.pdf#page=25 51389. The latter explicitly distinguishes the "big omega function" from the "little omega function", which counts distinct prime factors.

The signed prime Omega function is the "classical prime Omega function" characterized in the opening paragraph of section 2 of C. L. Samuels, Acta Arithmetica 205 (2022), no. 4, 341-370, https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.01012v2 51389. It is the unique homomorphism from the nonzero rationals under multiplication to the integers under addition that maps every prime to one; see bomuniq2 51417.

The signed prime Omega function can equivalently be obtained by extending the prime Omega function first to nonzero integers by absolute value, then to nonzero rationals by subtracting the denominator's value from the numerator's value; see bomabs 51399 and bomdiv 51392. This construction is an application of Theorems 1 and 2 in section 2, pp. 4-6, of J. K. Merikoski, P. Haukkanen, and T. Tossavainen, "Complete additivity, complete multiplicativity, and Leibniz-additivity on rationals", INTEGERS 21 (2021), A33, https://math.colgate.edu/~integers/v33/v33.pdf 51392.

No finite value at zero can preserve complete additivity for all inputs while every prime has value one: multiplying zero by a prime would require the value at zero to equal itself plus one. The prime Omega function can be extended to the nonnegative integers by assigning zero at zero, but this extension is not completely additive on its full domain. Additivity remains valid for nonzero inputs. Lean 4 Mathlib's ArithmeticFunction.cardFactors uses this convention; see that declaration in the module Mathlib.NumberTheory.ArithmeticFunction.Misc at https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/ 51392.

Another convention assigns infinity at zero and uses extended addition. Merikoski, Haukkanen, and Tossavainen discuss this convention in section 2, p. 2, immediately after equation (2) of the paper cited above. With addition extended so that infinity absorbs finite values and itself, the additivity law also holds when a factor is zero. Extending the signed prime Omega function in this way would require adding zero to its domain, an explicit branch at zero, an enlarged codomain, and extended addition. Its current definition excludes zero.

Wolfram Language's PrimeOmega has two factorization conventions. With the default setting on positive integers, it counts integer prime factors with multiplicity, giving the prime Omega function. With GaussianIntegers -> True, it counts Gaussian prime factors with multiplicity instead. The two counts can differ on the same positive integer: at two the integer count is one and the Gaussian count is two. Thus the Gaussian count does not extend the prime Omega function. Nonreal Gaussian-integer inputs automatically use Gaussian factorization. PrimeOmega is undefined at zero. See "Details and Options", "Scope / Numerical Evaluation", "Options / GaussianIntegers", and "Possible Issues" in the PrimeOmega documentation, https://reference.wolfram.com/language/ref/PrimeOmega.html 51392.

Another possible construction sums the absolute values of rational prime valuations. For a reduced fraction it adds the numerator and denominator multiplicities rather than subtracting them. It agrees with the prime Omega function on positive integers, but fails complete additivity under cancellation: a prime and its reciprocal each contribute one, whereas their product has count zero. This is a comparison of constructions, not an attribution of this alternative to the cited sources.

Samuels also extends the signed prime Omega function to all nonzero algebraic numbers, with rational values, in section 2, equation (2.1), of the 2022 paper cited above. For a nonzero algebraic input 𝑥 ∈ (𝔸 ∖ {0}), write 𝐷 = (degAA‘𝑥) and 𝐶 = ((minPolyAA‘𝑥)‘0). Here 𝐷 is the degree and 𝐶 the constant coefficient of its monic minimal polynomial. Two possible definitions assign to 𝑥, respectively, the values ((Ω‘(( -1↑𝐷) · 𝐶)) / 𝐷) and (Σ𝑝 ∈ {𝑞 ∈ ℙ ∣ (𝑞 pCnt 𝐶) ≠ 0}(𝑝 pCnt 𝐶) / 𝐷). The first uses the field norm from the field generated by the input; the second sums the prime valuations of the constant coefficient directly. These expressions describe the same extension, since the norm is (( -1↑𝐷) · 𝐶) and its sign does not affect prime valuations; see pcneg 17101. Both expressions use the algebraic degree df-dgraa 44414 and minimal polynomial df-mpaa 44415 from Stefan O'Rear's mathbox. They are not adopted here: mathbox-independent foundations and proofs of closure, rational agreement, and complete additivity remain to be supplied. The mathematical extension is unchanged under multiplication by an algebraic unit, an algebraic integer whose inverse is also an algebraic integer, and therefore defines a function on nonzero algebraic numbers modulo algebraic units. Theorem 2.1 treats extensions more generally. Complete additivity and the prime value force the positive square root of two to have value one half, so an algebraic extension cannot retain an integer codomain.

Samuels' Theorem 2.1 also characterizes rational-valued extensions on nonzero algebraic numbers modulo algebraic units through weighted logarithmic valuations. In equations (1.7)-(1.8), divide each local logarithmic absolute value by the logarithm of its rational prime and sum with rational weights. The weights are consistent: after a finite field extension, the weights above a place sum to its weight. Give each rational prime weight minus one to extend the signed prime Omega function. Unlike the norm construction, such an extension can distinguish conjugate algebraic inputs. The example after the proof of Theorem 2.3, section 5, pp. 18-19 of the cited arXiv version, uses the field generated by the imaginary unit. At its two places above five, replace the norm weights minus one half by minus one third and minus two thirds; keep the norm weights elsewhere and extend them by Lemma 5.1. This gives a distinct completely additive extension with rational values. These alternatives are not adopted here: their algebraic valuation foundations and extension proofs are outside the present rational construction.

For real-valued extensions of the signed prime Omega function to nonzero algebraic numbers modulo roots of unity, see C. L. Samuels, Canadian Mathematical Bulletin 69 (2026), no. 2, 609-627, section 1.2, pp. 611-612, Corollary 1.2 and the following "Prime Omega Function" example, https://doi.org/10.4153/S0008439525101343 44415. These likewise give functions on individual nonzero algebraic numbers by assigning each input the value of its class. They are unchanged under multiplication by a root of unity, a number whose positive integer power is one. This condition is weaker than invariance under all algebraic units. The continuous extensions there use the Weil-height norm. These algebraic extensions are not adopted here.

For the domain and integer-valued function property see bomdm 51384 and bomf 51388. Complete additivity on nonzero rationals is bommul 51391. Examples are bom1 51395, bomprm 51389, and bom4 51396. For positive-integer closure and characterization of primes see bomnncl 51390 and bomnn1 51412. (Contributed by Zhi Wang, 17-Oct-2026.)
